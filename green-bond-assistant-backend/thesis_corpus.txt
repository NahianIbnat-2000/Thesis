Abstract
This thesis tests whether political credibility signals shift the sovereign green bond Greenium
— the yield concession investors accept to hold a green-labelled sovereign bond relative to
a comparable conventional bond in emerging market economies. Using a twin-bond stacked
Difference-in-Differences design applied to India and Indonesia, the thesis tests three sequen-
tial hypotheses: whether daily GDELT V2Tone climate sentiment predicts Greenium changes,
whether signalling noise from contradictory government messaging compresses the Greenium,
and whether inaugural sovereign green bond issuance or a major internationally backed climate
commitment produces a structural level shift. All three hypotheses produce results that are not
statistically different from zero. A European robustness check recovers persistent greeniums of
−1.82 and−2.80 basis points in Germany and Denmark, confirming the methodology detects
greeniums where they exist. The findings are attributed to a market development threshold:
below a certain level of institutional investor depth and secondary market liquidity, credibility
signals cannot reach daily bond prices regardless of their genuine content.
Keywords:sovereign green bonds, greenium, difference-in-differences, political credibility,
emerging markets, GDELT, climate finance

Chapter 1
Introduction
In November 2022, at the G20 Summit in Bali, Indonesia received the largest climate finance
commitment ever directed at a single emerging market economy: USD 20 billion under the Just
Energy Transition Partnership (JETP) in exchange for an accelerated coal phase-down (IEA,
2022). Months earlier, India had become one of the first major South Asian economies to
issue a sovereign green bond, raising USD 1 billion at its inaugural auction to finance clean
energy projects (Reserve Bank of India, 2023). Both events were internationally visible, but
does it have an effect on the Greenium? The Greenium—the yield concession investors accept
to hold a green-labelled sovereign bond relative to a comparable conventional bond from the
same issuer—is the bond market’s real-time measure of whether governments’ environmental
commitments are believed (Ando et al., 2024; Zerbib, 2019). This paper aims to find if markets
price political credibility, credibility events should shift it. The explanation this thesis advances
is not that the signals are not credible, but that the market infrastructure needed to transmit
credibility signals into daily prices does not yet exist in emerging sovereign green bond markets.
Sovereign green bonds have grown from Poland’s inaugural issuance in December 2016 to
more than 40 sovereign issuers and USD 700 billion in cumulative global labelled debt by 2023
(Climate Bonds Initiative, 2024). For governments, the appeal is a credibility premium: if bond
markets reward genuine environmental commitment with lower borrowing costs, the Greenium
creates a direct financial incentive for climate policy ambition. The IMF (IMF, 2024), World
Bank (World Bank, 2023), and IFC (IFC, 2024) have all cited this mechanism in promoting
sovereign green bond programmes in emerging economies. Yet the empirical evidence for it
comes almost entirely from developed markets. Zerbib (2019) documents a mean Greenium
of−2 basis points for European corporate bonds. Ando et al. (2023, 2024) estimate−2 to−4
basis points for advanced economy sovereign pairs and−11 to−13 basis points for emerg-
ing market sovereigns, but their evidence is cross-sectional and focused on average pricing at
issuance. Kapraun et al. (2021) show that framework credibility drives Greenium magnitude
across 2,000 green bonds. No study has tested whether a discrete political event shifts the
Greenium dynamically in emerging market economies. That is the gap this thesis fills.
The central research question is:do political credibility signals shift the sovereign green
1
bond Greenium in emerging market economies?Three hypotheses operationalise this as a se-
quential causal chain: H1 tests whether the continuous daily information signal—climate media
sentiment—reaches bond prices. H2 tests whether the quality and coherence of that signal mod-
erates the Greenium. H3 tests whether a hard institutional commitment produces a structural
price shift when softer signals cannot. A null on H1 and H2 does not weaken H3; it sharpens
it, because it is consistent with the continuous information channel being inoperative at daily
frequency and directs attention to whether a single costly institutional act can cut through.
H1: Daily changes in GDELT V2Tone climate-news tone are positively associ-
ated with daily changes in the sovereign green bond Greenium in emerging market
economies (Baker and Wurgler, 2006; Bolla et al., 2019).
H2: Higher signalling noise—the daily standard deviation of V2Tone scores across
climate-relevant articles—is negatively associated with the Greenium, reflecting
investor discounting of contradictory government messaging (Gao and Schmittmann,
2022).
H3: Inaugural sovereign green bond issuance or a major internationally backed
climate credibility event produces a structural negative shift in the Greenium, iden-
tified by a stacked Difference-in-Differences design (Baker et al., 2022).
The primary sample is India and Indonesia—the two most significant emerging market
sovereign green bond programmes in Asia. A two-country design is a deliberate choice for
internal validity: it permits a precisely specified twin-bond DiD with identifiable treatment
events and genuine pre-treatment variation, rather than a cross-sectional specification across
a heterogeneous group of issuers where credibility, liquidity, and market depth would be dif-
ficult to disentangle. The trade-off is external validity: the findings should be interpreted as
evidence about these two specific credibility episodes in Asia, not as a general claim about
all emerging market sovereign green bond programmes. India’s treatment event is its second
sovereign green bond auction (27 October 2023); Indonesia’s is the JETP announcement at the
G20 Bali Summit (15 November 2022). Greenium is constructed using the twin-bond design
of Zerbib (2019) from daily yield data sourced from Refinitiv Eikon (Refinitiv Eikon, 2024).
Treatment effects are estimated using a stacked Difference-in-Differences design (Baker et al.,
2022) that corrects for the contamination bias in staggered treatment settings (Callaway and
Sant’Anna, 2021). Climate sentiment is measured using GDELT V2Tone (GDELT Project,
2023; Leetaru and Schrodt, 2013). Germany and Denmark serve as a methodological robust-
ness check—markets where the literature documents persistent greeniums of−1.8 to−2.8
basis points (Ando et al., 2023)—confirming that the absence of detectable post-event shifts in
Asia reflects market structure rather than a broken methodology.
This thesis makes three contributions. First, it provides the first quasi-experimental causal
evidence on whether political credibility events shift the sovereign green bond Greenium in
2
emerging market economies, applying a stacked DiD design (Baker et al., 2022) to a setting
where existing evidence is cross-sectional and developed-market concentrated (Ando et al.,
2024; Zerbib, 2019). Second, it integrates the credible commitment and signalling frameworks
from international political economy (North and Weingast, 1989; Tomz, 2007) with the green
bond pricing literature (Kapraun et al., 2021; Zerbib, 2019) and NLP sentiment measurement
(Bolla et al., 2019)—three bodies of work not previously combined in this context. Third, it
proposes a market development threshold argument: below a certain level of institutional in-
vestor depth and secondary market liquidity (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2024; Ehlers and Packer,
2017), credibility signals cannot reach daily bond prices regardless of their genuine content,
with direct implications for the policy case for sovereign green bonds in emerging economies
(IMF, 2024).
The thesis is organised as follows. Chapter 2 reviews the green bond pricing literature, the
theoretical frameworks from credible commitment theory and signalling, and the NLP tools for
sentiment measurement, before deriving the three hypotheses from the identified gaps. Chap-
ter 3 describes the data, twin-bond design, stacked DiD specification, and robustness checks.
Chapter 4 presents the primary results for India and Indonesia. Chapter 5 presents the European
robustness check and explains why the European null is economically different from the Asian
null. Chapter 6 synthesises the comparative findings and develops the market maturity inter-
pretation. The conclusion summarises the contributions, limitations, and directions for future
research.

Chapter 2
Literature Review
This chapter defines the core concepts, surveys the evidence on green bond pricing, develops
the theoretical frameworks from IPE and information economics, identifies the gaps in the
existing literature, and derives the three research questions that structure the thesis.
2.1 Core Concepts and Definitions
Green Bond.A fixed-income instrument whose proceeds are contractually designated for
projects with defined environmental benefits, issued under the International Capital Market As-
sociation Green Bond Principles (ICMA, 2021). Total global green bond issuance exceeded
USD 700 billion in 2023, with emerging market sovereign issuance alone reaching USD 27
billion in 2022, up from virtually nothing in 2015 (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2024). Despite
rapid growth, the market remains concentrated among a small number of issuers, and the pric-
ing dynamics of sovereign green bonds in emerging economies are not yet well understood
(Ando et al., 2024).
Greenium.The yield differential between a green bond and an otherwise comparable con-
ventional bond from the same issuer, defined as:

A negative Greenium means investors accept a lower yield to hold the green-labelled instru-
ment, reflecting their valuation of the environmental commitment beyond the bond’s purely
financial characteristics (Zerbib, 2019). Measuring the greenium requires controlling for ma-
turity, coupon, liquidity, and credit quality; the dominant methodological solution is the twin-
bond design (Zerbib, 2019), in which each green bond is matched to a conventional bond from
the same issuer with comparable maturity.
Investor Sentiment.A belief about future asset values not fully justified by observable
fundamentals (Baker and Wurgler, 2007). In fixed-income markets, sentiment has been shown
to predict bond yield movements beyond what macroeconomic fundamentals alone explain
4
(Baker and Wurgler, 2006; Tetlock, 2007). Baker and Wurgler define investor sentiment as
beliefs about future cash flows and risks; GDELT V2Tone, by contrast, measures the aver-
age linguistic tone of news articles across a global media database. This thesis therefore uses
GDELT V2Tone as a proxy for climate-news tone—a measure of the information environ-
ment investors are exposed to—rather than as a direct measure of investor sentiment in the
Baker–Wurgler sense. Bolla et al. (2019) validate GDELT-based tone measures as predictors
of sovereign bond spread movements, supporting their use as an information-channel proxy.
Signalling Noise.The degree of internal contradiction in a government’s public environ-
mental messaging, operationalised here as the daily standard deviation of V2Tone scores across
climate-relevant articles. Spence (1973) establishes that a signal’s informativeness depends not
only on its magnitude but on its clarity: contradictory signals from the same sender—a finance
ministry issuing green bonds while a state energy agency approves new coal projects—reduce
the receiver’s ability to distinguish genuine commitment from strategic communication and
should, under the theory, compress the credibility premium investors are willing to pay.
2.2 Green Bond Pricing: What the Evidence Shows
Zerbib (2019) establishes the foundational result using 135 matched corporate and suprana-
tional bond pairs: green bonds trade at a mean yield discount of approximately−2 basis points,
a small but statistically significant negative Greenium driven by pro-environmental investor
preferences. Hachenberg and Schiereck (2018) confirm a similar magnitude for European cor-
porate bonds. Baker et al. (2018) document a larger greenium of−6 to−9 basis points for US
municipal green bonds, attributed to tax-advantaged demand from dedicated ESG investors.
These findings establish that the green label carries a real, if modest, market price in developed
bond markets.
For sovereign green bonds, Ando et al. (2023, 2024) provide the most comprehensive cross-
country evidence, estimating an average Greenium of−2 to−4 basis points in advanced
economies and−11 to−13 basis points in emerging market economies across 332 matched
sovereign pairs from 2014 to 2023. The larger emerging market Greenium is attributed to
asymmetric information rather than market depth: investors in riskier markets assign greater
value to the credibility certification the green label provides (Ando et al., 2024). Omondi et al.
(2026) confirm a Greenium of approximately−10 basis points for emerging market green-
labelled sovereign bonds using coarsened exact matching across 44 countries, and find that
the Greenium is especially large for speculative-grade bonds and during periods of financial
volatility.
The quality of the green framework drives the magnitude of the Greenium. Kapraun et al.
(2021) examine over 2,000 green bonds and show that externally verified, ICMA-aligned bonds
carry significantly larger Greeniums than unverified ones. This is not a label effect—it is a cred-
ibility effect. Markets price the quality of the commitment, not merely its existence. The ESG
5
investor base reinforces this: Gianfrate and Peri (2019) show that green bonds attract larger
order books at issuance than comparable conventional bonds, consistent with excess demand
from investors who value the green designation intrinsically. In thin markets with limited ESG
institutional participation, this demand is absent and the greenium becomes volatile, as this
thesis documents for India and Indonesia. Febi et al. (2018) add that liquidity differences
between green and conventional bonds partially offset the demand premium, with less liquid
green bonds trading at yield premiums that reduce the net greenium.
2.3 Theoretical Frameworks
Three unresolved tensions in this literature motivate the present thesis. First, the evidence on
whether the greenium is a credibility effect or a liquidity effect is not settled: Zerbib (2019) and
Ando et al. (2024) attribute the greenium to pro-environmental investor preferences, while Febi
et al. (2018) show that liquidity differentials partially offset the demand premium—making
it difficult to separate genuine credibility pricing from market microstructure. Second, the
mechanism by which political events translate into greenium movements has not been identified
causally: Kapraun et al. (2021) show that framework quality matters cross-sectionally, but no
study has tested the dynamic response to a discrete political event using quasi-experimental
variation. Third, the emerging market evidence (Ando et al., 2024; Omondi et al., 2026) is
almost entirely cross-sectional—it shows that greeniums exist on average, but cannot answer
whether political credibility shocks shift them at high frequency. This thesis addresses the
second and third gaps directly: it provides the first quasi-experimental evidence on whether
political credibility events shift the sovereign greenium in emerging markets, and tests the
causal channel through which such events would operate.
2.3.1 Credible Commitment Theory
Whether bond markets price sovereign green commitments depends on whether those com-
mitments are believed. Kydland and Prescott (1977) establish the time-inconsistency prob-
lem: governments have incentives to renege, and forward-looking investors discount accord-
ingly. The sovereign greenium is the market’s real-time assessment of whether the commit-
ment will hold. Tomz (2007) shows empirically that reputational track records are directly re-
flected in sovereign borrowing costs. North and Weingast (1989) identify institutional structure
as the commitment technology: embedding commitments in hard-to-reverse arrangements—
independent oversight, mandatory reporting, external verification—raises the cost of defection.
Berg et al. (2016) confirm empirically that environmental performance reduces sovereign bond
spreads in emerging markets.

2.3.2 Asymmetric Information and Signalling
Spence (1973) develops the canonical signalling model: in environments with asymmetric in-
formation, credible communication requires a costly signal—one that only a genuinely com-
mitted sender would choose to send. A cheap signal conveys no information in equilibrium
because uncommitted senders mimic it. Applied to sovereign green bonds, a government gen-
uinely committed to environmental transition can more easily bear the institutional costs of the
green bond programme—framework documentation, rigorous annual reporting, reputational
exposure from internationally visible issuance—than a government engaged in greenwashing.
The Greenium reflects the market’s estimate of the probability the signal is genuine. Gao and
Schmittmann (2022) formalise this in a green bond pricing model under asymmetric infor-
mation, deriving that the equilibrium Greenium is larger when verification requirements are
stronger and when the issuer’s credibility type is more uncertain—consistent with the larger
Greenium observed in emerging markets by Ando et al. (2024) and Kapraun et al. (2021).
Mosley (2003) and Ballard-Rosa et al. (2021) establish that international financial markets are
effective monitors of sovereign fiscal and institutional commitments, with spreads incorporat-
ing political risk assessments—extending the signalling framework to the international political
economy context in which sovereign green bonds operate.
2.4 Climate Sentiment and NLP in Financial Markets
Tetlock (2007) establishes that negative media sentiment predicts downward stock market pres-
sure using a Wall Street Journal column as the sentiment source. Tetlock et al. (2008) extend
this to firm-level news, showing that qualitative article content predicts future earnings surprises
and abnormal returns. Baker and Wurgler (2006) provide the broader framework: investor sen-
timent systematically affects asset returns, particularly for assets—like green bonds—whose
fundamental values are hard to assess objectively. In sovereign fixed-income markets, GDELT-
based sentiment measures have been validated as predictors of yield spread movements: Bolla
et al. (2019) show that GDELT V2Tone for Italy, Spain, and France predicts sovereign yield
spreads relative to Germany within days, confirming that large-scale news databases capture
information relevant to bond investors.
For green bonds specifically, Pi˜neiro-Chousa et al. (2021) show that social media sentiment
positively influences green bond market returns, confirming the sentiment channel operates in
this asset class. Pham (2021) documents that climate policy news sentiment predicts green
bond fund flows in European markets. However, both studies use developed market aggregate
indices rather than individual sovereign pairs in emerging economies. The GDELT sentiment
channel has not been tested in thin, nascent sovereign green bond markets where the dedicated
ESG investor base is small and daily trading volume can be near zero—the gap this thesis
addresses.

2.5 Sovereign Green Bonds in Emerging Market Economies:
The Gap
Despite the rapid growth of emerging market sovereign green bond issuance—from virtually
nothing in 2015 to USD 27 billion in 2022 (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2024)—the academic lit-
erature has not kept pace. Ando et al. (2024) and Omondi et al. (2026) provide cross-sectional
Greenium estimates but do not test the causal effect of specific political events on the Gree-
nium. Ehlers and Packer (2017) describe structural differences between emerging and devel-
oped sovereign green bond markets but do not test whether these differences block the credi-
bility transmission mechanism. Wang et al. (2020) find a small positive Greenium for Chinese
corporate green bonds but do not address the sovereign context or the political economy of gov-
ernment commitment credibility. No existing study applies a quasi-experimental DiD design
to test whether political credibility events shift the sovereign Greenium in emerging market
economies.
Three structural features of emerging market sovereign green bond markets are relevant to
this thesis. First, secondary market liquidity is substantially lower than in developed markets,
making daily prices noisy residuals of thin trading rather than clean equilibrium signals (Ehlers
and Packer, 2017). Second, the dedicated ESG institutional investor base—the pension funds,
insurance companies, and central banks whose mandate-driven demand sustains Greeniums
in Germany and Denmark—is largely absent in most emerging economies (Climate Bonds
Initiative, 2024). Third, foreign currency denomination in some emerging market issuances
introduces currency risk that can dominate yield spread movements on volatile days (Longstaff
et al., 2011). Together, these features suggest that the mechanisms through which credibility
signals reach Greenium prices in developed markets may not yet be operative in emerging
economies—the central question this thesis tests.
2.6 Identification Strategy
Estimating the causal effect of a political event on the Greenium requires a design that identifies
the differential response of the green bond relative to its conventional twin. The Difference-in-
Differences estimator (Angrist and Pischke, 2009) provides this by comparing the before-after
change in the Greenium for the treated pair to the simultaneous change for an untreated control.
Larcker and Watts (2020) apply this design to US municipal green bonds and find no Greenium
absent ESG investor demand differentiation, illustrating the sensitivity of the design to control
group quality.
When treatment is staggered—as it is here, with Indonesia treated in November 2022 and
India in October 2023—the standard two-way fixed effects DiD estimator is biased because
already-treated units contaminate the control group for later-treated units (Baker et al., 2022;
8
Callaway and Sant’Anna, 2021). The stacked DiD design formalised by Baker et al. (2022)
addresses this by constructing event-specific sub-samples in which only not-yet-treated units
serve as controls. Roth (2022) provides the formal framework for pre-testing the parallel trends
assumption through a joint Wald F-test on pre-event period dummies, the approach adopted in
Chapter 3.
2.7 Research Questions
Three gaps motivate the research questions. First, the causal effect of political credibility
events on the sovereign Greenium in emerging market economies has not been tested with
quasi-experimental rigour (Ando et al., 2024; Omondi et al., 2026). Second, the credible com-
mitment and signalling frameworks from IPE (North and Weingast, 1989; Spence, 1973; Tomz,
2007) have not been operationalised using daily Greenium data. Third, GDELT-based climate
sentiment (Bolla et al., 2019) has not been applied to this specific pricing question in emerging
economies.
These gaps generate three research questions that form a sequential causal chain rooted in
Spence’s (1973) signalling hierarchy:
RQ1 (Information channel):Do daily changes in climate sentiment predict daily changes
in the sovereign green bond Greenium in emerging market economies? Following Tetlock
(2007) and Baker and Wurgler (2006), this tests whether the continuous low-cost information
signal—media coverage of the government’s environmental stance—reaches bond prices at
daily frequency.
RQ2 (Signal clarity):Does the internal coherence of climate media coverage moderate the
Greenium? Following Spence (1973) and Gao and Schmittmann (2022), this tests whether
contradictory government messaging compresses the credibility premium below what senti-
ment tone alone predicts.
RQ3 (Institutional commitment):Does a major sovereign credibility event—an inaugural
green bond issuance or an internationally backed climate finance commitment—produce a
structural level shift in the Greenium? Following Tomz (2007), this tests whether the costliest,
most institutionally embedded signal produces effects that softer signals cannot.
The three questions connect through the signalling logic of Spence (1973): an undetectable
effect on RQ1 motivates RQ2, and undetectable effects on both motivate RQ3. Findings that
are not statistically different from zero across all three channels would establish that emerg-
ing market sovereign green bond markets, at their current stage of development, do not show
detectable pricing of political credibility signals at daily frequency—a substantive finding for
both the green finance literature and the policy community. The research questions are framed
at the level of emerging market sovereign green bond markets as a class. India and Indone-
sia are the empirical cases; Germany and Denmark serve as the methodological benchmark,
9
confirming that the absence of detectable effects in Asia reflects market structure rather than a
broken methodology (Ando et al., 2023).

3.1 Sample Selection and Twin-Bond Design
The primary sample is two Asian sovereign green bond pairs: India and Indonesia. They are
the natural subjects for this question. India issued its inaugural sovereign green bond in January
2023, becoming the first major South Asian government to do so. Indonesia had already entered
the market in 2018 with offshore green sukuk, and in 2022 it attracted the largest single climate
finance commitment ever directed at an emerging market. These are not peripheral cases. They
are the two most consequential sovereign green bond programmes in Asia, and they occur at
times when the credibility signal is salient and theoretically motivated.
Each sovereign green bond is paired with a conventional bond from the same issuer and
comparable maturity, following Zerbib (2019). The yield spread between the two is the Gree-
nium. Pairing within the same sovereign issuer controls for credit risk and country risk; what
remains is the pricing of the green label itself. Germany and Denmark are added as a robustness
check using exact twin bonds—identical coupon and maturity—providing an upper bound on
what the methodology can detect (Ando et al., 2023). Table A.1 in Appendix A lists all four
bond pairs.

permanence: the government is committed to recurring green issuance, not a one-off gesture.

3.4 Identification Strategy
3.4.1 First-Differencing
Sovereign bond yields are persistent. The Durbin-Watson statistic for the greenium in levels is
approximately 0.44 for both Asian pairs—well below the 1.5 threshold that would be consistent
with the absence of positive serial correlation. Regressing levels on levels in this environment
produces spurious associations and inflatedt-statistics. The remedy is first-differencing: re-
placing each variable with its daily change within each bond pair:
∆Xi,t =X i,t −X i,t−1,for allX, within pairi(3.3)
Differencing is applied within each pair group, so no cross-pair contamination occurs. The
Durbin-Watson statistic rises from approximately 0.44 (levels) to 2.0 (differenced), confirming
serial correlation is removed. Daily rather than monthly data is used because: (1) it enables the
event study parallel trends test; (2) it supports the stacked DiD window design; and (3) it tests
the information channel at the frequency investors actually trade. Monthly data would absorb
within-month dynamics and conflate the treatment event with time fixed effects.
After first-differencing, all regressors are standardised to zero mean and unit standard de-
viation. This means every coefficient can be read as: a one-standard-deviation change in the
daily change of that variable is associated with a ˆβ-unit change in∆Greenium, in percentage
points. Coefficients across variables are directly comparable.

3.4.3 The Stacked DiD
The pooled M5 specification uses Indonesia—already treated in November 2022—as a de-facto
control for India in the post-October 2023 window. This is the contamination bias documented
by Baker et al. (2022): already-treated units bias the treatment effect when used as controls for
later-treated units.
The stacked DiD addresses this cleanly. Following Baker et al. (2022), two event-specific
sub-datasets are constructed, each covering a window of±180 calendar days around the re-
spective treatment date. Each sub-dataset—called a stack—uses only not-yet-treated units as
controls.

treatment event (that occurs in October 2023, well outside this window), so India is a clean,
not-yet-treated control. Stack A is a valid DiD in the Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) sense:

Stack A ATT is a credible causal estimate for Indonesia.

eleven months and cannot serve as a clean control. Stack B contains India alone and is a pre-
post comparison, not a DiD. It is reported for transparency only.

The placebo test shifts both treatment dates simultaneously by−3,+3, and+6 months. All

Results: Asia (India and Indonesia)
The central question of this thesis is whether bond markets price political credibility signals
in sovereign green bond greeniums. The answer, for India and Indonesia, is No—not under
any specification tested. But a null result is not an empty result. The data tell a coherent story:
Asian sovereign green bond markets are priced by global risk appetite, not by domestic political
commitment. This chapter works through that story from the raw descriptive patterns through
the main regressions, the stacked DiD, and the robustness checks.
4.1 What the Data Look Like Before Any Regression
Before fitting a single model, it is worth noting two key numbers. Two numbers stand out
immediately. India’s mean greenium over the sample period is+0.50 basis points—positive,
meaning Indian investors did not, on average, accept a yield concession to hold the green-
labelled bond. Indonesia’s mean is−6.91 basis points—a genuine persistent negative gree-
nium, but one that comes with a standard deviation of 4.12 basis points. Seven basis points of
average compression, with daily swings of four basis points in either direction. That is not a
stable pricing signal. It is a noisy series dominated by something other than the green label.
The greenium correlates at−0.249 with log(VIX)and−0.474 with log(FX rate). Global
macro conditions, not the green label, are driving daily variation—consistent with a thin market
where risk appetite determines whether investors hold the green instrument at all.

in a liquid, informationally efficient market.

4.6 Economic Interpretation: What the Null Means
A null result carries information. The null H3 finding for India and Indonesia does not mean
nothing happened after these treatment events. It means bond markets did not price what hap-
pened. That distinction is important for both economics and policy.
Asian sovereign green bond markets in 2022 to 2024 appear to be thin, globally integrated,
and macro-dominated. The institutional investor base is small and secondary market liquidity
is limited, based on available evidence. In this environment, the daily greenium is driven by
the global risk cycle—captured by VIX—not by domestic political developments. When VIX
rises, investors exit the less liquid green leg first and the greenium compresses. This is a story
about risk appetite and market depth, not about the green label.
The treatment events are credible political signals by any reasonable standard. But credi-
bility signals require a receiver—investors who monitor these events and trade on them. The
evidence suggests that investor base in both Asian markets was not doing this at daily frequency.
This is a substantive null finding for the green finance literature. It is consistent with the
interpretation that the credibility premium—the yield concession investors accept for a credible
green commitment—may require a minimum level of market development before it manifests
22
in observable prices. Whether India and Indonesia had reached that threshold, or whether
the null reflects other factors such as measurement limitations or insufficient statistical power,
cannot be determined from this design alone.

5.4 What the European Results Establish
Two things, and only two things.
First: the methodology works.The twin-bond pipeline correctly identifies and measures
persistent greeniums of 1.8 to 2.8 basis points in Germany and Denmark, consistent with what

broken pipeline.
Second: the European null is economically different from the Asian null.In Europe,
the null arises because the greenium is a stable equilibrium established at inaugural issuance.
The DiD correctly finds no post-issuance dynamic shift because there was none. In Asia, the
null arises because there is no stable greenium to detect—the greenium is volatile, macro-
dominated, and structurally thin. These are two different nulls with two different economic
explanations. The distinction matters for how the findings should be interpreted and what
policy implications follow. Chapter 6 develops this interpretation in full.

Start with what is the same. The Post Treatment coefficient is statistically indistinguish-
able from zero in every country and every specification. No government action tested in this
thesis—not an inaugural issuance, not a second auction, not a USD 20 billion international
climate finance pledge—produced a detectable structural shift in the daily greenium. That is a
consistent finding across four sovereign pairs, two continents, and six years of data.
Now look at what is different. Germany and Denmark have mean greeniums of−1.82
and−2.80 basis points, with standard deviations of roughly 2 basis points. The green label is
persistently and stably priced. India has a positive mean greenium—investors require a yield
pick-up, on average, to hold the green bond. Indonesia has a large mean of−6.91 basis points
but a standard deviation of 4.12 basis points: daily swings nearly as large as the mean itself.
VIX is the dominant driver in both Asian markets. It is not significant in either European model.
These are structurally distinct environments that happen to produce the same DiD output for
entirely different reasons.
The European null is the null of a stable equilibrium. The Asian null is the null of an absent
signal. There is no stable greenium to break. Daily prices are overwhelmingly determined
by global risk appetite—when VIX rises, investors rotate out of the less liquid green leg first,
compressing or eliminating the greenium. A political commitment has no channel through
which to shift an equilibrium that does not exist.
6.2 Why the Information Channels Are Silent
Before turning to the H3 null, it is worth accounting for the M1 and M2 results, which test the
information and signal clarity channels. Sentiment is null in both Asian markets. Signalling
noise is null. Neither the tone of climate media coverage nor the contradictoriness of that
coverage predicts daily greenium changes in India or Indonesia. The answer is the same market
structure argument that explains the H3 null, applied at finer temporal resolution.
For climate-news tone to move the greenium, investors must observe, update, and trade
within the same day. In a market where daily volume can be zero and the marginal buyer has no
green mandate, this chain breaks. Signalling noise follows the same logic: monitoring cross-
agency contradictions requires dedicated ESG institutions who actively trade on credibility
assessments. Both channels are theoretically sound. One interpretation is that the market is not
yet sufficiently structured to reveal them at daily frequency—though measurement limitations
in the GDELT proxy cannot be ruled out as an alternative explanation.
6.3 A Threshold Interpretation
The pattern across all three channels—sentiment, noise, and institutional commitment—points
to a single explanation. One interpretation consistent with the findings is that the credibility
29
premium in sovereign green bond markets may not be a smooth function of political credibility
that rises continuously as governments say and do more credible things. The results are con-
sistent with a threshold level of market development being required before credibility signals
manifest in observable daily prices—though the design cannot directly test this mechanism or
rule out alternatives such as differences in investor composition, political salience, or media
coverage quality across markets.
Below the threshold—if it exists—the investor base may be too thin and undifferentiated
to transmit daily tone signals, monitor cross-agency coherence, or sustain a stable greenium
against global risk shocks. Above the threshold—consistent with what is observed in Germany
and Denmark—the greenium appears to be established at inaugural issuance by ESG-mandated
institutional investors whose demand is structural, not tactical. The dominant daily driver in
Europe is consistent with mandate rather than VIX.
The two nulls therefore carry different meanings. In Asia, undetectable effects tell us about
market structure, not signal quality—the signals may be genuine but the market cannot price
them yet. In Europe, the null means the credibility question was resolved at programme launch;
subsequent signals add nothing because the inaugural issuance already incorporated everything
the market needed.
6.4 What Moves the Greenium in Asia, and Why It Matters
The one consistently significant finding in the Asian regressions is the VIX coefficient:−0.0035
to−0.0048 percentage points per standard deviation, significant at the five percent level across
every specification and robust across all three standard error assumptions. Asian sovereign
green bond markets are globally integrated at the liquidity level but not at the credibility level.
A one-standard-deviation increase in the daily change of log(VIX)is associated with a 0.35
to 0.48 basis point compression of the greenium; domestic political commitments leave no de-
tectable trace. The evidence is consistent with the marginal investor being risk-sensitive rather
than mandate-driven—holding the green bond for yield in calm times and exiting when fear
rises.
The evidence suggests that ESG investors who would reprice in response to a second auc-
tion or a JETP announcement are not yet present in sufficient numbers. The investor who does
appear to be present responds to VIX rather than credibility signals, consistent with risk-driven
rather than mandate-driven participation.
6.5 Policy Implications
The findings support two policy conclusions, each tied directly to a specific result.
Conclusion 1: market depth must precede credibility signalling.The VIX dominance
result implies that the marginal Asian green bond investor is risk-sensitive and not credibility-
30
responsive. Governments in India, Indonesia, and similarly positioned emerging markets can-
not manufacture a credibility premium by signalling more loudly or more credibly: the market
infrastructure needed to price credibility signals does not yet exist. The productive policy pri-
ority is building the receiver—expanding the domestic institutional investor base through green
bond purchase programmes, creating explicit ESG mandates for pension funds and insurance
companies, supporting market-making to improve secondary market liquidity, and developing
the price discovery mechanisms that allow dedicated green investors to form a stable demand
base.
Conclusion 2: inaugural issuance is the credibility event that matters in mature mar-
kets.The European results show that the greenium is established at inaugural issuance and
then stable. Subsequent political commitments, additional auctions, and ongoing programme
communications do not shift it. This implies that the marginal return to credibility investment
is highest at programme launch—when the market is forming its view of the programme for the
first time—and falls sharply thereafter. Governments in more developed markets, or emerging
market governments designing future programmes, should invest heavily in the pre-issuance
institutional framework: the green bond framework document, the allocation reporting pro-
cess, the independent verification mechanism. These determine the inaugural greenium. What
comes after cannot easily move it.
6.6 Limitations
Four limitations qualify the findings and must be stated plainly alongside the results.
Indonesia’s short pre-treatment window.The conventional twin bond began trading in
September 2022, leaving approximately 55 trading days before the November 2022 JETP treat-
ment. The formal parallel trends test passes atF(7,75) =0.740,p=0.64, but the pre-event
coefficients show a downward drift that the test lacks the power to distinguish from zero. The
null H3 finding for Indonesia should be read with this caveat.
No pre-issuance greenium data for Germany and Denmark.Both European green bonds
were issued for the first time at the treatment date. The parallel trends assumption cannot be
empirically assessed. European identification rests entirely on the exact twin-bond design and
the assumption that issuance dates were exogenous to prevailing market conditions.
N=2clusters in all pooled specifications.The cluster-robust variance estimator is asymp-
totically invalid with fewer than 20 to 30 clusters (Cameron and Miller, 2015). The spuri-
ously significant Post coefficient under pair-level clustering—p=0.004 in Table 4.3, against
p=0.593 under HC3—illustrates the severity of the downward bias withN=2. Future work
on this question will require data from more sovereign green bond pairs before valid clustered
inference is possible.
GDELT V2Tone as a climate sentiment proxy.V2Tone measures the average linguistic
tone of news articles mentioning climate-related themes for a given country-day. It is a climate-
31
news tone proxy, not a measure of investor sentiment, ESG fund positioning, or green bond-
specific news flow. The absence of detectable H1 and H2 effects may reflect the limitations
of this proxy as much as the absence of a true information-channel effect. A purpose-built in-
vestor sentiment measure—institutional survey data, ESG fund flow indices, or climate-specific
financial news sentiment scores targeted at fixed-income desks—would permit a more precise
test.
Finally, the market development threshold interpretation, while consistent with the Euro-
pean comparison, is not directly identified in this design. The two-country Asian sample can-
not distinguish the threshold explanation from alternative accounts—including differences in
investor composition, political salience of green commitments, media coverage quality, or the
possibility that the treatment events themselves were not sufficiently salient to generate a de-
tectable price response. Distinguishing these mechanisms would require cross-sectional varia-
tion in market depth across a larger sample of sovereign green bond issuers, which this thesis
does not attempt. The threshold argument is therefore offered as a direction for future research
rather than a directly tested causal claim.

Chapter 7
Conclusion
This thesis tested whether political credibility signals shift the sovereign green bond greenium
in emerging market economies. Across all three hypotheses and all specifications, the post-
event coefficients are not statistically distinguishable from zero at conventional significance
levels. Daily climate-news tone does not predict greenium changes in India or Indonesia (H1).
Signalling noise is not detectably associated with the greenium (H2). Neither India’s second
sovereign green bond auction nor Indonesia’s USD 20 billion JETP commitment produced a
statistically detectable structural shift in the greenium (H3). These results do not prove the true
effects are exactly zero; they establish that, given this sample, frequency, and measurement, the
study does not recover clear evidence of a repricing effect.
The one consistently significant result is VIX. A one-standard-deviation increase in the
daily change of log(VIX)is associated with a 0.35 to 0.48 basis point compression of the
Asian greenium, significant at the five percent level across all specifications and robust to HC3,
PCSE, and pair-level clustering. Global risk appetite—not domestic political commitment—is
the dominant pricing force in both Asian sovereign green bond markets.
The European robustness check confirms the methodology works. Germany’s mean gree-
nium is−1.82 basis points and Denmark’s is−2.80 basis points, consistent with Ando et al.
(2023). The European DiD also finds no post-issuance dynamic shift—but for a different rea-
son: the greenium is a stable level established at inaugural issuance, and first-differencing
removes it. The Asian null and the European null look the same in a regression table but arise
from entirely different market structures.
The central argument is a market development threshold. In Asian sovereign green bond
markets, the dedicated ESG institutional investor base is limited, secondary market liquidity is
thin, and prices are dominated by global risk conditions (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2024; Ehlers
and Packer, 2017). Credibility signals cannot reach the greenium not because they are weak,
but because the market infrastructure to price them does not yet exist. In Europe, the greenium
is established at inaugural issuance by a deep, mandate-driven investor base and then stable.
The VIX dominance result and the two distinct nulls together tell the same story: credibility
can only be priced where investors are positioned to price it.
33
Three contributions follow. First, this thesis provides the first quasi-experimental evidence
on whether political credibility events shift the sovereign green bond greenium in emerging
markets, using a stacked DiD (Baker et al., 2022). Second, it integrates credible commitment
and signalling theory from IPE (Spence, 1973; Tomz, 2007) with the green bond pricing lit-
erature (Ando et al., 2024; Zerbib, 2019) and NLP measurement (Bolla et al., 2019). Third,
it distinguishes two economically distinct nulls—the European stable-equilibrium null and the
Asian absent-infrastructure null—with direct implications for how null results in emerging mar-
ket green bond studies should be interpreted.
Two policy conclusions follow directly from the results. The VIX dominance finding im-
plies that market depth must precede credibility signalling: governments seeking a greenium
should first build the receiver—domestic ESG investor mandates, market-making programmes,
secondary market liquidity—before optimising the signal. The European evidence implies that
the inaugural issuance is the credibility event that matters most once that infrastructure exists;
subsequent political signals add little because the market has already priced the commitment at
programme launch. Four limitations qualify these conclusions: Indonesia’s short 55-day pre-
treatment window; the absence of pre-issuance greenium data for Europe;N=2 bond pairs
making clustered inference unreliable; and GDELT V2Tone as a climate-news proxy rather
than a direct investor sentiment measure. Future research should test whether the credibility
premium emerges as Asian markets mature, and whether corporate green bonds in the same
markets show a different pattern.
