When governments say "green", do bond markets believe them?

A green bond is a promise: the money will fund something green. If investors believe the promise, they accept a slightly lower yield, a discount called the greenium. But a promise is only words, so this project asks a sharper question: when a government signals its climate commitment, through speeches, announcements, pledges, and the climate conversation around it, does that talk actually move the price investors pay? Or does credibility need more than words?

This is my MA thesis, submitted for the Economics, Data & Policy degree at Central European University. The interactive tools below turn its findings into something anyone can explore.

The finding, in one line

Credibility needs a receiver.

In India and Indonesia, no credibility signal (climate-news tone, signalling noise, or a green-bond auction) moved the greenium. The channel returns a clean null. Only global risk appetite (VIX) drives the daily price. The greenium is real in deep markets like Germany and Denmark, so the null is a feature of thin markets, not a flaw in green bonds.

Why it matters. Most green-bond pricing evidence comes from developed markets. This is one of the first twin-bond studies of emerging-market sovereign green debt, and it shows that in thin markets, credibility signals do not yet reach bond prices. The European benchmark confirms the method works, so the null reflects market depth, not a failure to measure.

The design

How the question was tested

Each sovereign green bond is paired with a maturity-matched conventional twin from the same issuer. The daily yield gap between them is the greenium, in basis points.

The estimator is a twin-bond stacked difference-in-differences anchored on two political shocks, with climate-news tone measured from GDELT V2Tone, and HC3 / panel-corrected standard errors throughout.

These are precise nulls: coefficients sitting near zero with p-values around 0.4 to 0.6, not noisy non-results. The two-country sample limits statistical power, which the thesis states plainly.
